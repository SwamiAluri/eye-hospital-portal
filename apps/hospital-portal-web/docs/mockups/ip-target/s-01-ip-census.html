What opens when you click a row

What the product does today. Clicking a row renders PatientQuickPanel, which mounts fixed inset-0 z-40 bg-black/30 backdrop-blur-[2px] — a scrim that dims and blurs the entire hospital dashboard — and slides a fixed right-0 top-0 bottom-0 z-50 w-[380px] sheet over the top of it, covering the application’s own navigation. Selecting a patient in a worklist is a reading action, and this is the visual language of a modal interruption. That mismatch is the whole reason it reads as unprofessional.

Recommendation, and why not a modal

You asked whether this should become a modal popup or a new page. My recommendation is neither on its own — it should be a docked split pane that resizes the list, escalating to the full patient record at S-10 when someone needs depth. A modal would make the current problem worse rather than better.

OptionVerdictReasoning
Modal popup
centred dialog over the list		 Reject A modal is a contract: it says make one decision, then leave. A patient summary is reference information consulted while working the list, and a coordinator moves through forty patients in a session. A modal blocks the list completely, cannot be compared against the row it came from, and would need dismissing forty times. It is also the pattern the current design is already accidentally imitating, so it would not fix the perception at all.
Full page only
navigate to the patient record		 Right, but not as the only answer The correct destination for depth, and it already exists as S-10. As the only response to a row click it is punishing for triage: navigate, read one number, go back, lose your scroll position and your filters, repeat. High-flow day care is the case this list exists to serve.
Overlay drawer
today’s behaviour		 Reject Floats above the page with a scrim, hides the list it belongs to, sits over the app chrome, and squeezes clinical, financial and audit content into 380 px. It also holds selection in React state only, so a refresh loses the patient and the view cannot be sent to a colleague.
Docked split pane → full page 3 Recommended The pane is part of the layout: the table narrows, nothing is covered, nothing is dimmed, the navigation stays reachable. It answers the triage question in place, and carries one clear route into the full record for everything else. This is the worklist-and-chart split every serious clinical system settles on, because the two tasks are genuinely different.

Six changes that make it read as clinical software

#ChangeWhy it matters here
1 Remove the scrim and the overlay. The pane lives in the grid and the table column narrows to make room. No fixed, no z-50, no blur, and it starts below the app header. Nothing is hidden and nothing is suspended. This single change removes most of the “popup” feeling on its own.
2 Add a patient identification banner that never scrolls and never truncates: full name, UHID, age, sex, date of birth, allergy and risk alerts, and the operating eye. This is the piece that is genuinely missing rather than merely ugly. Right now laterality appears as the two letters OD inside a grey subtitle line, next to the procedure. In an eye hospital the operating side is the field a wrong-site event turns on, so it is given its own bordered block, spelled out as RIGHT with OD underneath, and never conveyed by colour alone.
3 Widen to 440 px and order the content by clinical risk, in fixed zones that are always in the same place. 380 px forces truncation of exactly the things that must not be truncated. Fixed ordering means staff learn where to look instead of reading the panel each time.
4 Put the selection in the URL as ?patient={id} rather than in useState. A refresh keeps the patient, the browser back button behaves, and a coordinator can send a colleague a link to the patient they are asking about. Today none of that works.
5 One primary action in a pinned footer, chosen by state and permission, with the rest demoted. The panel currently offers several similarly weighted calls to action, including four competing routes into billing. A footer that says exactly one thing is what makes it feel decided rather than assembled.
6 Escalate, do not expand. The pane answers triage; the full record at S-10 answers everything else, reached by one clear control. Resisting the urge to grow the pane is what keeps it usable. Audit history, implants and the full billing ledger belong on a page with room for them.

Ordered by the question “what would stop this patient progressing in the next hour”. Zones 1 to 3 are always present for every persona; zones 4 to 6 are scoped by permission, as set out in S-02.

Where the pane goes when the screen is narrow

One part of this banner is not free. Laterality and the identifiers cost nothing to add: PatientJourneyRowDto already returns eyeOperated, uhid, patientName, patientDob and patientAge, so the operating-eye block and the identity line are a pure frontend change. The alert strip is not. The only allergy fields that exist today are allergyKnown and allergyDetails on BeforeAnaesthesiaSectionDto, captured at the WHO sign-in inside theatre — far too late to warn a ward nurse, and attached to a checklist rather than to the patient. A patient-level clinical alert source has to be added and exposed on the journey row. Until it is, the strip must render nothing rather than an empty state: showing “no known allergies” when nothing has been wired up would turn a missing feature into a false reassurance, which is worse than the panel we are replacing. The “No known allergies” chip in variant 2 above is therefore only correct once a real answer to that question is being stored.
The distinction worth keeping. A scrim is correct when the interface really is asking the user to stop and deal with one thing — collecting a payment, confirming a cancellation. It is wrong when they are simply looking at a patient in a list they are working through. Today’s panel uses the strong pattern for the weak case, which is why it feels wrong even though every individual field on it is reasonable.
